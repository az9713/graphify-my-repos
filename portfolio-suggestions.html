The honest read

Five clusters, one distribution problem

Sorted roughly by size, the portfolio is: Claude Code harness engineering — skills, hooks, agent fleets, memory systems, and the through-line of everything you build; tutorial forks of other people's repos, around 180 of them; interactive HTML explainers covering physics, mathematics, and interpretability; AI media pipelines for video, image and audio; and trading bots, thirteen separate ones.

The technical range is genuinely wide. There is validated computational fluid dynamics running in a browser, a superoptimizer that proves shortest-program floors by exhaustive search, a limit-order-book simulator that models queue position, an adversarially-verified compendium of Putnam proofs, and a WebGPU ray-traced puzzle game. That is not a hobbyist's shelf.

The thing worth naming first

Your stars land on the wrong repos. The 56★, 34★, 16★, 14★ and 12★ entries are almost all clones or ports of work that was already popular before you touched it. Meanwhile superoptimizer-foundry, lbm-wind-tunnel, market-microstructure-observatory and proof-foundry — all original, all harder — sit at zero.

That is a distribution problem, not a quality problem. It is fixable in an afternoon, and it is why the first suggestion below is the cheapest one on the list.

The second structural observation: the portfolio has almost no contact with anything outside a computer. Across 448 repos there is not one sensor, motor, satellite image, protein, or physical measurement. Everything is simulated, documented, or generated. That is the single largest unexplored surface, and Track B leans into it hard.

A2

Collapse thirteen trading repos into one honest research platform

The best backtester you've written is sitting under nothing.

market-microstructure-observatoryregimebothmm-regime-traderHMM-regime-terminalself-improving-trading-agentTradingAgentslong-short-equity-fundtechnical-analysis-frameworkbollinger-noirta-terminalyet-another-stock-terminalautoresearch-stock-tradingturboquant-tutorial

Thirteen repos, thirteen partial answers. The crown jewel is market-microstructure-observatory: it models latency, fees, and — unusually — queue position, meaning it tracks where your order actually sits in line at a given price rather than pretending you get filled instantly. Almost no hobby backtester does this, and it is frequently the entire difference between a strategy that looks profitable and one that is.

Right now that engine runs nothing. The ambitious consolidation: make it the execution layer beneath every strategy in the portfolio, demote the HMM regime classifier from "the system" to one risk gate among several, and let the autoresearch loop from autoresearch-stock-trading propose candidates that must survive a pre-registered statistical gauntlet before anyone celebrates.

That gauntlet is the intellectual core, and it's three tests. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio asks: given that I tried N strategies, how good would the best one look by luck alone? White's Reality Check answers the same question by bootstrapping the whole search rather than assuming a distribution. The Harvey–Liu haircut then shaves the reported performance down to what survives that correction. In plain terms: test five hundred ideas and some will look brilliant purely by chance — these methods estimate how brilliant chance alone gets, so you can subtract it.

The hard part: being willing to publish the result you get. "We generated 500 strategies and none survived multiple-testing correction" is a more valuable repository than another green equity curve, and it is the one nobody ships.
CostHigh — long agent loops, heavy data plumbing
First moveLift the microstructure fill engine into a standalone package with a clean strategy interface
PayoffTwelve dead repos become one that a quant would actually read
A3

Make proof-foundry machine-checked

Right now the referee is a language model. Make it a compiler.

proof-foundryno-proof-left-behindmulti-agent-paper-auditten-math-cs-advances

Today proof-foundry holds eight Putnam proofs plus eight Erdős surveys, written by a 110-agent fleet and checked adversarially by other agents. It is persuasive. It is not certain, because every participant in the loop is the same kind of system making the same kinds of mistakes.

Port the proofs to Lean 4 with Mathlib, where a successful lake build is the proof — a kernel a few thousand lines long checks every inference, and no amount of eloquence gets past it. Then build the genuinely novel part: an autoformalization loop where an agent reads the informal proof, drafts Lean, and iterates against the compiler's error messages until it type-checks or the agent gives up.

Publish the pass rate honestly. It will be low — current research puts competition-level autoformalization well short of solved — and the shape of the failures is the interesting artifact. Which steps do LLMs skip when nobody is checking? That's answerable here, and almost nowhere else.

The hard part: Mathlib's naming conventions and the sheer distance between "obviously true" and "accepted by the kernel." Budget for the agent to fail loudly and often.
CostHigh — iterative compile loops burn tokens fast
First moveFormalize the single easiest Putnam problem by hand first, to calibrate the gap
BonusEnters proof assistants, a domain absent from all 448 repos
A4

Take superoptimizer-foundry to a real ISA — and find a bug in LLVM

Brute force on a toy instruction set was the warm-up.

superoptimizer-foundrycheat_code_compilerpolyglot-crap

You proved shortest-program floors by exhaustive enumeration over a pinned 8-bit ISA — a real result, and a defensible one because exhaustion admits no doubt. But it caps out fast: the search space grows exponentially with program length, so brute force dies around four or five instructions.

Next rung: a real RV32I subset (the RISC-V base integer instruction set), with the search driven by an SMT solver rather than enumeration. Z3's bitvector theory can answer "does there exist a shorter program equivalent to this one on all 2³² inputs?" directly, without visiting each candidate. This is the lineage of Souper, LLVM's own superoptimizer, and STOKE, which searches stochastically instead.

The concrete target that makes this ambitious rather than academic: find one peephole rewrite that LLVM's optimizer misses, prove it correct for all inputs, and submit it upstream as an InstCombine patch. A merged compiler patch is checkable by strangers and permanent — a very different artifact from a zero-star HTML page.

The hard part: encoding instruction semantics into SMT precisely enough that the solver's "equivalent" means what you think it means. Flags and undefined behavior are where these projects die.
CostMedium — solver does the work, you do the encoding
First moveEncode ten RV32I instructions in Z3 and reproduce one rewrite LLVM already knows, as a correctness check
PayoffAn upstream patch in a compiler millions of people use
A5

Merge the two fluid solvers into a validated CFD benchmark suite

You solved the same problem twice. Now prove the solution converges.

gpu-fluid-lablbm-wind-tunnelwebgpu-ai-labmeep

gpu-fluid-lab is a WebGPU Navier–Stokes solver validated against the Ghia et al. lid-driven cavity benchmark. lbm-wind-tunnel is a lattice-Boltzmann solver validated on drafting and V-formation flight. Two codebases, two validation stories, one underlying physics.

Unify them behind one solver interface with a real validation harness and four standard benchmarks: the lid-driven cavity (Ghia — the field's hello-world), flow past a cylinder checked against the Strouhal-versus-Reynolds curve, the backward-facing step against Armaly's reattachment-length data, and Taylor–Green vortex decay, which has an exact analytic solution so the error is known to the digit.

Then do the thing that essentially no browser simulation does: a grid-convergence study. If your scheme claims second-order accuracy, halving the grid spacing should cut the error by a factor of four. Plot error against spacing on log axes and show the slope is 2. That single graph is the difference between "looks like fluid" and "is trustworthy enough to assign to a class."

The hard part: the convergence plot is where solvers get caught lying. Boundary conditions that are only first-order accurate will flatten the slope and force you to fix them — which is exactly the value.
CostMedium — the physics exists, the harness is the work
First moveRun Taylor–Green on the existing solver at three grid resolutions and plot the slope. Find out what you actually have.
AudienceTeachers and students — the one group that needs validated browser sims and can't find them

B1

The physical world

Highest contrast of anything on this page. Zero of 448 repos touch a sensor or a motor.

new domain: embedded / control↔ f1-digital-twin↔ evtol-studio↔ mars-sample-return↔ gyroscope-physics

You have built an entire shelf of simulated bodies — an F1 car with combined-slip tire models, a six-degree-of-freedom eVTOL, a Mars transfer planner, a gyroscope textbook. Not one of them ever had to be right about a real object. In simulation you write the physics; in hardware the physics writes you.

The project: a hardware-in-the-loop tuning rig. An ESP32 or Pi Pico, an inertial measurement unit, and a PCA9685 driving a two-axis servo gimbal. An agent tunes a stabilization controller against the actual plant and must discover, from data alone, the errors no simulator volunteers:

  • The microcontroller's clock drifts, so your 100 Hz loop isn't 100 Hz
  • The servo is nonlinear near its travel limits and has backlash in the gear train
  • The gyroscope has a bias that wanders with temperature — it reads rotation while sitting still
  • The PCA9685's internal oscillator runs a few percent fast out of the box, so requested pulse widths aren't delivered ones

Every one of those needs a calibration constant that no amount of clean code replaces. That is the entire lesson, and it is the one lesson 448 software repos cannot teach.

Why it's the strongest pick on the page: reality is the grader. There is no way to write a convincing README about a gimbal that oscillates. Roughly $40 of parts, and it is the only project here an LLM cannot talk its way through.
CostLow in tokens — the bottleneck is parts shipping, not usage
First moveOrder the parts today. Pico + MPU-6050 + PCA9685 + two servos.
StretchLet the agent run the tuning loop unattended overnight and log every calibration it discovers
B2

A storage engine, tested against a disk that lies

Database internals: absent from all 448 repos, and exactly where generated code falls apart.

new domain: systems / storage

Write an LSM-tree key-value store in Rust — the write-optimized structure behind RocksDB, Cassandra and LevelDB, where writes land in memory, get flushed to sorted files, and are compacted in the background. Building the tree is a known quantity; that's not the ambitious part.

The ambitious part is the test harness: deterministic simulation testing, in the style FoundationDB pioneered and TigerBeetle refined. You replace the operating system with a simulation — a fake clock you control, and a fake disk that actively betrays you. It tears writes in half mid-flight. It silently ignores an fsync and then loses the data. It reorders operations that you assumed were sequential. Every run is driven from a random seed, so any crash you find replays byte-identically until you fix it.

Then layer on Jepsen-style consistency checking: record every operation, and afterwards verify that the history you observed could have been produced by some valid sequential execution. Not "did it crash" but "did it ever tell me something untrue."

Why this domain specifically: crash-consistency is where LLM-written code quietly falls apart, because the bugs only appear at the intersection of failure timing and buffering. Getting it right is therefore an unusually strong signal — and the seeded simulator is what makes it possible for one person to get right at all.
CostHigh — real systems work, no shortcuts
First moveBuild the lying-disk simulator before the storage engine. The order matters.
AdjacentPairs naturally with B3 if you want a full systems arc
B3

Raft, specified in TLA+ and checked against the implementation

Plenty of people implement Raft. Almost nobody closes the loop.

new domain: distributed systems / formal methods

Implement Raft consensus — the algorithm that lets a cluster of machines agree on a sequence of operations even when some of them crash or the network splits. Then write the TLA+ specification and run the TLC model checker over it, which exhaustively explores every interleaving of messages and failures up to a bounded size and proves that no reachable state violates your safety property.

Then the step that makes it rare: trace validation. Instrument your running Rust or Go implementation to emit a log of state transitions, and check that log against the spec — confirming the code actually refines the design it claims to implement. Most projects have a spec and code that drifted apart within a month; very few can demonstrate they didn't.

The hard part: TLA+ thinks in states and actions rather than in code, and the translation is genuinely a different mode of thought. That difficulty is the reason it's worth doing.
CostMedium — the spec is small, the mental shift is not
First moveModel-check a two-node leader election before writing a line of implementation

B4

A zkSNARK from scratch, with the explainer to match

Applied cryptography is a one-repo stub. This is your strongest format aimed at a genuine gap.

new domain: applied cryptographypost-quantum-cryptography↔ rigorous-explainer

Build a small zero-knowledge proof system end to end — a way to convince someone that you know a solution without revealing it. The pipeline: express a computation as an arithmetic circuit, flatten it into R1CS (rank-1 constraint system, a set of multiplication constraints in matrix form), then produce and verify proofs with Groth16 or a PLONK-style scheme. Along the way you implement elliptic-curve pairings — a bilinear map that lets a verifier check a polynomial identity without ever seeing the polynomial.

Then wrap it in the format you're demonstrably best at: a rigorous interactive explainer with every symbol defined before use, hand-drawn figures, and the derivation shown rather than asserted. There is a real shortage of material that goes all the way from "here is what a polynomial commitment is" to code that runs — most explanations stop at the metaphor, and most implementations assume you already understand the metaphor.

The hard part: the trusted setup and pairing arithmetic are unforgiving; an off-by-one in the field arithmetic produces proofs that verify for the wrong reason. Test against a reference implementation early.

First moveGet a Schnorr proof of knowledge working first, then build up to circuits

B5

Do protein language models actually know which mutations are dangerous?

No biology anywhere in 448 repos. This one runs on a laptop.

new domain: computational biologymedical-report-analyzer↔ autoresearch-llm-molecule-bench

Pull ClinVar — a curated public database of genetic variants annotated as disease-causing or benign — and gnomAD, which catalogues variation observed across large populations of ostensibly healthy people. Run ESM-2, a protein language model trained on evolutionary sequence data, and test whether its zero-shot likelihood ratio separates the two groups. The model never saw a disease label; it only learned what protein sequences evolution tends to produce. The claim is that "evolutionarily implausible" tracks "medically dangerous."

The published result says it largely works. Your contribution is the stress test, and it's where the interesting answer lives:

  • Does it hold for rare variants, where the evidence is thinnest and clinical need is greatest?
  • Does it hold for proteins with shallow evolutionary alignments — few known relatives, so less signal to learn from?
  • How much of the apparent skill is just conservation, recoverable from a simple alignment score without any neural network at all?

That last question is the one that matters and the one most papers under-answer. A clean negative — "the fancy model buys you less than you'd think over a 30-year-old baseline" — is genuinely publishable and a far better repo than a confirmation.

The hard part: label leakage. ClinVar annotations correlate with how well-studied a gene is, so a model can look skilled by tracking research attention rather than biology. Control for it explicitly.
CostMedium — data wrangling dominates; a small ESM checkpoint fits a consumer GPU
First moveReproduce the headline separation on a single well-studied gene before scaling
Honest outcomeThe baseline comparison is the paper. Run it first, not last.
B6

Watch land change from orbit

No geospatial work at all — and the imagery is free.

new domain: remote sensing / geospatialterra-pulse

Sentinel-2 and Landsat archives are openly available through STAC APIs, which let you query decades of imagery by location and date without downloading a petabyte. Two framings, both real:

Land-cover change detection over a region you care about — with honest handling of everything that breaks the naive version. Clouds and their shadows must be masked or they register as change. Seasonal phenology means a deciduous field genuinely "changes" every autumn without anything happening. And sensor calibration drifts across a multi-year archive, so raw pixel values from 2015 and 2025 are not directly comparable without radiometric correction.

Or the civic version: urban heat islands from Landsat's thermal infrared bands, cross-referenced against tree canopy cover. Converting radiance to land-surface temperature requires emissivity estimation and atmospheric correction — real physics with a real answer — and the output is a map with actual weight in a planning conversation.

The hard part: the corrections are the project. Anyone can difference two images; almost nobody handles the three confounds above, which is exactly why most amateur change-detection maps are wrong.

B7

Stop explaining interpretability and do it

Two explainers about the field. Zero artifacts in it.

new domain: mechanistic interpretability (hands-on)the-J-spacejacobian-lens-labnanochatnanochat-with-claude

You've written two well-made explainers about Anthropic's Jacobian-lens research and never trained an interpretability artifact yourself. Given how clearly this is the area you find most interesting, that gap is the loudest one in the portfolio.

Train a sparse autoencoder on a small open model — Pythia-160M or GPT-2 small, comfortably within a consumer GPU. The problem it solves: a single neuron in a language model typically fires for many unrelated things at once, because the model packs more concepts than it has dimensions. An SAE re-expresses those tangled activations in a much wider, mostly-zero basis, so each learned feature stands a chance of meaning one thing.

Then do the step most hobby SAE projects skip entirely: causal intervention. Pick a feature you think means something, ablate it, and show a specific predicted change in the model's behavior. That is the whole difference between "this feature correlates with X" and "this feature causes X" — and it is the difference between commentary and practice.

The hard part: resisting the pull of cherry-picked feature galleries. A dozen features with verified causal effects beats a thousand with suggestive names, and honest reporting of the ones that turned out to mean nothing is what makes it credible.
CostLow in tokens — the cost is GPU hours, not agent turns
First moveTrain on a single mid-layer residual stream and find one feature you can steer with
EffectMoves you from commentator to practitioner in the field you clearly care most about
B8

A language where units are types and conservation laws are checked

You've hinted at this twice and never followed through.

new domain: language design / type theoryphysics-type-checkernoether-symmetry-compilerlambda-calculus-tutor

Two repos in your history gesture at this and stop short. Build the real thing: a small language with a genuine type checker where dimensional analysis is enforced by the type system. Adding meters to seconds is a compile error, not a lost Mars orbiter. Dividing a distance by a time yields a velocity type, derived automatically, not annotated by hand.

Then the part that makes it novel rather than merely nice: symmetry annotations on a Lagrangian, from which the corresponding conserved quantity falls out at compile time via Noether's theorem. Declare that your system is invariant under time translation, and the compiler tells you energy is conserved — and flags any expression in your simulation that violates it. Translation invariance gives momentum; rotational invariance gives angular momentum.

Nobody has shipped this well. Units-as-types exists in fragments across several languages; the Noether half exists in nobody's compiler. It sits precisely at the intersection of your physics fluency, your compiler curiosity, and your explainer craft.

The hard part: keeping the type system decidable once you allow rational exponents on dimensions. Constrain the ambition early or it becomes a research project.
CostMedium — a small language is a weekend; a good one is longer
First moveUnits-only first, checked on a projectile problem. Add Noether once that's solid.
OriginalityHighest on the page — genuinely nobody owns this
